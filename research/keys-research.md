# Choosing a Key: What the Tradition Claims, What Actually Survives, and What an Engine Should Do

Research deliverable for motif-engine. Everything proposed for the engine at the end is a **CANDIDATE** — a pool entry awaiting ear ratification, not a fact about what sounds good. The historical and empirical material is reported as scholarship, with honesty about where it is folklore.

## 1. The historical key-characteristics tradition

From roughly 1680–1850, European musicians widely believed each key had an intrinsic emotional character. The best-known list is from Christian Friedrich Daniel Schubart's *Ideen zu einer Ästhetik der Tonkunst* (written 1784/85 in prison, published posthumously 1806) — a book Beethoven owned and annotated ([Houston Symphony](https://houstonsymphony.org/recommended-listening-musical-keys-their-meanings/), [IMSLP](https://imslp.org/wiki/Ideen_zu_einer_%C3%84sthetik_der_Tonkunst_(Schubart,_Christian_Friedrich_Daniel))). Earlier entries in the same tradition: Marc-Antoine Charpentier's key-character list in *Règles de composition* (c. 1682) and Johann Mattheson's extensive key descriptions in *Das neu-eröffnete Orchestre* (1713). The definitive modern study is Rita Steblin, *A History of Key Characteristics in the 18th and Early 19th Centuries* ([Steblin, 2nd ed. 2002](https://www.amazon.com/History-Characteristics-18th-Early-Centuries/dp/1580460410)), which documents both the lists and the era's fierce disagreements about them (the Rameau–Rousseau and Marpurg–Kirnberger temperament controversies).

### Schubart's 1806 list (Steblin's translation, as circulated via the Western Michigan University theory page)

Source: the widely mirrored WMU "Affective Musical Key Characteristics" page ([legacy.wmich.edu](https://legacy.wmich.edu/mus-theo/courses/keys.html)) and the Houston Symphony excerpt ([houstonsymphony.org](https://houstonsymphony.org/recommended-listening-musical-keys-their-meanings/)). Wording below is Steblin's translation, lightly compressed for the flat-key grotesques.

| G major | Everything rustic, idyllic, lyrical; every calm and satisfied passion; tender gratitude for true friendship and faithful love. |
| E minor | Naïve, womanly, innocent declaration of love; lament without grumbling; sighs with few tears. |
| D major | The key of triumph, of hallelujahs, of war-cries, of victory-rejoicing. Marches and heaven-rejoicing choruses live here. |
| B minor | The key of patience, of calmly awaiting one's fate, of submission to divine dispensation. |
| A major | Innocent love, satisfaction with one's state of affairs; hope of seeing one's beloved again; youthful cheerfulness, trust in God. |
| F♯ minor | A gloomy key; it tugs at passion as a dog biting a dress. Resentment and discontent. |
| E major | Noisy shouts of joy, laughing pleasure, not-yet-complete delight. |
| C♯ minor | Penitential lamentation, intimate conversation with God; sighs of disappointed friendship and love. |
| B major | Strongly colored, announcing wild passions; anger, rage, jealousy, fury, despair. |
| G♯ minor | Grumbling, a heart squeezed until it suffocates; wailing lament; everything struggling with difficulty. |
| F major | Complaisance and calm. |
| D minor | Melancholy womanliness; the spleen and humours brood. ("Melancholy, brooding, lost hope" in the Houston Symphony gloss.) |
| B♭ major | Cheerful love, clear conscience, hope, aspiration for a better world. |
| G minor | Discontent, uneasiness, worry over a failed scheme; bad-tempered gnashing of teeth; resentment and dislike. |
| E♭ major | The key of love, of devotion, of intimate conversation with God. |
| C minor | Declaration of love and at the same time the lament of unhappy love; all languishing, longing, sighing of the love-sick soul. |
| A♭ major | Key of the grave. Death, grave, putrefaction, judgment, eternity lie in its radius. |
| F minor | Deep depression, funereal lament, groans of misery, longing for the grave. |
| D♭ major | A leering key, degenerating into grief and rapture; it cannot laugh, but it can smile. |
| B♭ minor | A quaint creature, often dressed in the garment of night; surly; mocking God and the world; "preparation for suicide sounds in this key." |
| G♭ / F♯ major | Triumph over difficulty; the free sigh of relief after surmounted hurdles; echo of a soul that has struggled and finally conquered. |
| E♭ minor | The soul's deepest distress, brooding despair, blackest depression. "If ghosts could speak, their speech would approximate this key." |

Note the systematic pattern Steblin identifies: **sharp keys trend bright/active, flat keys trend dark/solemn**, and the further from C in either direction, the more extreme the affect. That pattern — not the specific poetry — is the durable cultural residue.

**Honesty check:** the lists disagree with each other. Charpentier calls E minor "effeminate, amorous, plaintive" but Mattheson calls it deeply pensive and grief-stricken; authors even flip individual keys between joyous and mournful. Schubart's list won the meme war largely because it was vivid and Beethoven-adjacent, not because it was measured.

## 2. Why the tradition existed — and what survives in 12-TET

### Why it was once objectively real

Before equal temperament, keyboards used **meantone** and later **well temperaments** (Werckmeister, Kirnberger, Vallotti…). In these tunings the sizes of major thirds and fifths *differ from key to key*: C major had nearly pure thirds; A♭ or F♯ major had wide, beating, restless intervals. Well temperament was explicitly designed to keep near-meantone purity in common keys while making all 24 keys usable — so remote keys really did sound rougher and more tense on the same instrument ([Duffin, *How Equal Temperament Ruined Harmony*, ECHO/UCLA](https://echo.humspace.ucla.edu/issues/how-equal-temperament-ruined-harmony-and-why-you-should-care-by-ross-w-duffin/); [Steblin](https://www.amazon.com/History-Characteristics-18th-Early-Centuries/dp/1580460410); [Daniel Adam Maltz on historical tuning](https://www.danieladammaltz.com/tcp/historical-tuning-rediscover-classical-sound-world)). A given key was therefore a *timbral* choice, not just a transposition. Add that concert pitch itself drifted (A ≈ 415–435 Hz historically vs. 440+ today), so Schubart's "D major" is not even the same set of frequencies as ours — a second reason intrinsic-key claims don't transfer.

### What actually survives in modern 12-TET

In equal temperament, every key is an exact transposition of every other. On a fixed-pitch synth there is *no* intrinsic difference between C major and D♭ major except absolute frequency. What remains real:

1. **Absolute register.** Transposing up or down changes where melody and bass sit, and register genuinely changes affect: higher/faster reads happier and brighter, lower reads darker and heavier ([Poon & Schutz-type analysis of Bach & Chopin 24-piece sets](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4629484/); [affective qualities across pitch registers, Frontiers in Psychology 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00153/full)). Notably, major-key pieces in the Bach/Chopin corpora average ~2 semitones higher and ~29% faster than minor-key pieces — composers use register as an emotion cue, not tonic letter.
2. **Instrument idiom.** Keys that engage open strings resonate more and finger more easily on strings and guitar (violin family open strings G–D–A–E → sharp keys; guitar open strings E–A–D–G–B–E → E, A, D, G); B♭/E♭ brass and winds make flat keys (B♭, E♭, F) natural for bands and orchestral brass writing; on piano, black-key-rich keys like D♭ and F♯ have their own hand topography (Chopin famously taught B major first as most natural for the hand) ([Why instruments are in different keys](https://www.partykeys.com/blogs/news/why-instruments-are-in-different-keys); [Transposing instrument, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transposing_instrument); [TalkBass thread on B♭ brass](https://www.talkbass.com/threads/why-are-brass-instruments-tuned-in-bb.1138262/page-2)). This is why real corpora cluster: Spotify's analysis of 30M tracks found G major (10.7%) and C major (10.2%) on top, with G/C/D/A covering over a third of all songs — guitar-and-piano-friendly keys, and majors far outnumbering minors (A minor top minor at 4.8%) ([Spotify Insights](https://insights.spotify.com/dk/2015/05/06/most-popular-keys-on-spotify/) via [Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/music/whats-the-most-popular-music-key-spotify/), [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/a-chart-of-the-most-commonly-used-keys-shows-our-actual-1703086174)).
3. **Vocal range.** For sung music, the tonic is chosen to keep the melody's tessitura inside the singer's comfortable compass; this is the dominant practical driver in pop/musical theatre and is why the same song is published in multiple keys.
4. **Modulation geography.** The tonic you start in determines where the relative minor, the ♭VI, the half-step lift, etc. *land in register* across sections. Key choice is partly a routing decision for the whole piece, not a single-section affect decision.
5. **Listener pitch memory.** Ordinary listeners carry surprisingly fine implicit absolute-pitch memory: most people detect when a familiar recording is shifted by even one semitone, and remember key and tempo of frequently heard recordings ([Schellenberg, Weiss et al. 2019, *Music & Science*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2059204319857198); [AP judgments of familiar melodies generalize across timbre and octave, *Memory & Cognition* 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-023-01429-z)). So a leitmotif's *absolute* pitch level is part of its identity for returning listeners — a real, empirically grounded reason to keep a theme in "its" key across a soundtrack.
6. **Convention/association.** Because the tradition existed, composers kept writing triumph in D, pastorales in F/G, funerals in flat minors — so listeners' learned associations partially self-fulfill through repertoire, not acoustics.

## 3. Empirical studies on key perception in equal temperament

- **Powell & Dibben (2005), "Key-Mood Association: A Self-Perpetuating Myth," *Musicae Scientiae* 9(2):289–311** ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/102986490500900208)). ~75% of questioned musicians *claimed* key-mood associations, and their claims strongly correlated with the 18th-century sharp=bright / flat=mellow scheme — but in listening tests participants **could not** identify mood from key or key from mood, and transposing a piece did not change its perceived mood on an equal-tempered keyboard. Conclusion in the title: the association is a culturally transmitted myth for modern fixed-pitch instruments.
- **Schellenberg, Weiss, Peng & Alam (2019)** ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2059204319857198)): fine-grained implicit memory for the key and tempo of familiar recordings; key effects clearer than tempo, plausibly via categorical pitch perception. (Supports leitmotif-pitch-stability, *not* intrinsic key affect.)
- **Bach/Chopin 24-piece-set analysis** ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4629484/)): major-key pieces sit ~2 semitones higher and 29% faster — emotion is carried by mode + register + tempo, paralleling emotional speech cues.
- **Register/timbre affect** ([Frontiers 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00153/full)): affective quality of instrument sounds varies systematically with pitch register — the mechanism by which "key" choice still matters (because it moves register).
- **Parncutt (2014)** on the origins of major/minor emotional connotations ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1029864914542842)): the *mode* dichotomy has multiple plausible roots and is robust — unlike per-tonic affect.

**Honest summary:** the science says mode, register, tempo, and timbre carry the affect; the tonic letter carries essentially none of it on equal-tempered fixed-pitch instruments — except through learned association and pitch memory of specific pieces.

Patterns worth noting, with confidence labels:

- **B♭ major fanfares are a brass-idiom artifact.** Williams' heroic Bb (and the concert-band tradition generally) follows B♭/E♭ brass instruments' natural harmonics ([transposing-instrument sources above](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transposing_instrument)). Kondo's Zelda Bb plausibly inherits the same orchestral-fanfare convention even on an NES chip, where no such constraint exists — convention outliving its cause.
- **Chip-era caveat (recollection, not verified here):** on the NES APU, pitch is a period register, so tuning accuracy degrades in high registers; chip composers sometimes chose keys/registers for tuning accuracy rather than affect. Treat as unverified until checked against NESdev documentation.
- **"D minor = epic/doom" is a modern production-music convention** (Megalovania, Mozart's Requiem, Beethoven 9, Toccata & Fugue, plus the Spinal Tap joke "D minor is the saddest of all keys"). Zimmer-style epic writing frequently sits in D minor territory (e.g., the D-minor–F–Am–G loop of "Journey to the Line" — [UJAM tutorial](https://www.ujam.com/tutorials/how-to-create-an-epic-hans-zimmer-style-film-score/)), partly because low D is where cellos/basses (open D string) and big synth patches growl best. I found no primary-source quote from Zimmer declaring a favorite key; the association is corpus-level, not confessed. Label: convention, medium confidence.
- **Pop/indie-game corpus gravity:** Spotify's 30M-song data (G, C, D, A majors dominate) reflects guitar/keyboard ergonomics ([Spotify Insights](https://insights.spotify.com/dk/2015/05/06/most-popular-keys-on-spotify/)). Toby Fox writing in a DAW with piano roll shows a similar plain-key gravity (lots of white-key and one-to-two-flat keys in Hooktheory's Undertale tabs), but I did not verify a full key census of the Undertale OST — building one from the repo's imported corpus would be more reliable than web transcriptions.

## 5. The decision procedure a practitioner actually uses — as engine axes

What working composers actually do, distilled honestly: **they pick the mode and the register; the tonic letter mostly falls out of instrument/vocal practicality, modulation planning, and habit.** For a fixed-pitch digital engine, every one of Schubart's poetic distinctions collapses — except where it survives as corpus convention the audience has learned.

Proposed axes for motif-engine — **all CANDIDATES pending ear verdicts:**

1. **Axis 1 — Mode/scale first (the affect lever).** Major/minor/modal choice carries nearly all key-related emotion (Powell & Dibben; Parncutt). The engine should treat mode selection as the semantic decision ("battle = minor/Dorian, town = major/Mixolydian…") and keep it separate from tonic selection.
2. **Axis 2 — Register second (the only acoustically real tonic effect).** Choose the tonic *so that* melody and bass land in each voice's sweet band for the chosen timbres (melody roughly C4–C6 bright vs. lower = darker; bass tessitura clear of mud). Concretely: pick target melody-center and bass-floor pitches per scene mood, then solve for the tonic that places scale degrees there. Register, tempo, and timbre are the empirically supported emotion cues.
3. **Axis 3 — Tonic by corpus convention (tie-break, not affect).** Where register admits several tonics, weight by corpus practice: the Undertale-adjacent palette (C, F, B♭, G, D minor, A minor per verified examples above) and the pop-corpus priors (G/C/D/A majors, A/E minors). This buys idiomatic familiarity for free and costs nothing. A per-key "Schubart flavor tag" may be kept as *naming metadata only* — never as a sound-quality rule.
4. **Axis 4 — Leitmotif pitch identity.** Keep each recurring motif at a fixed absolute pitch level across tracks/sections unless a transposition is a deliberate dramatic statement — grounded in implicit absolute-pitch memory (Schellenberg et al. 2019). Megalovania living permanently in D minor is the model.
5. **Axis 5 — Modulation geography.** Choose the *home* tonic with the destination keys in mind: plan section moves (relative, parallel, IV, ♭VI, chromatic mediant, semitone lift) and verify every destination still satisfies Axis 2's register bands. The home key is a routing decision for the whole piece.
6. **Axis 6 — Idiom emulation (conditional).** Only when a patch imitates a physical instrument: bias toward its idiomatic keys (strings → G/D/A/E; brass/band → B♭/E♭/F; guitar textures → E/A/D/G) so open-string-like voicings and characteristic registers come out right. On abstract synth patches this axis is off.

**One-line honest answer to the owner's question:** keys are not "good at" anything intrinsically in 12-TET — mode and register are; the tonic letter is convention, ergonomics, and memory. Decide mode → register band → then let corpus convention and modulation plans pick the letter.
